Preface

The evolution of personal computers and the expansion of access to them and the Internet have transformed how we learn and teach. Specifically regarding the study of nature through scientific methods, the increased availability of free tools for data analysis and easier access to data are among the most significant changes in the past 20 years. Today, it is relatively common, even among early-career scientists, to discuss new packages, programming code, public data repositories, and reproducibility of analyses. For this to happen, in addition to technological advances, many people also needed to be willing to teach how to use these tools. This book is one such contribution to the continued advancement of computational methods education, with a specific focus on ecological data analysis using R.

Beyond the breadth of topics covered (from t-tests to geospatial analyses, including phylogenetic diversity), another distinctive aspect of this book is the authors’ perspective on how code should be a consequence of the questions research seeks to answer (see Chapter 1). This perspective results in a book that consistently connects ecological theory, scientific methods, quantitative analyses, and programming. This is done explicitly through clear and didactic examples that present real data and context, one or more possible research questions, predictions related to those questions and underlying theory, and variables that could be used in the analyses. The explanatory text is interwoven with organized and clear code snippets and figures, making the chapters fluid and dynamic, especially for those who like to execute code while reading. It is like a guided practical class. A third notable aspect is that many analyses are also explained conceptually. For example, when the t-test is introduced, it is not only presented with code, but also includes explanations of how the test works and its assumptions. This encourages readers to understand what happens behind the code.

When I began teaching ecological data analysis to undergraduate ecology students in 2012 at UNESP Rio Claro, I used to tell my classes that we had a great privilege: a textbook specific to our field and in Portuguese—“Princípios de Estatística em Ecologia” (Gotelli and Ellison)—and a set of notes from which I drew instructions and exercises in R, also in Portuguese. These notes, developed by Diogo B. Provete, Fernando R. da Silva, and Thiago Gonçalves-Souza, were the embryo of this book (see Chapter 1 for details), and one of the students I told about this privilege was Maurício Vancine. Therefore, it is a great personal satisfaction to write this preface—not only because I know four of the five authors and have used an earlier version of the material, but also because I consider this book one of the most comprehensive sources on data analysis in R. I suggest bookmarking the book link in your browser.

Although the authors did not divide the chapters into sections, we can consider two main groups. In the first group (Chapters 1 to 6), we are introduced to the general structure of the book, its objectives, and how R works. In this group, we learn everything from installing R and RStudio (Chapter 3) to producing clear and informative graphics (Chapter 6). In the second group (Chapters 7 to 15), we encounter specific analyses currently used in ecology, ranging from simple linear models (Chapter 7) to complex geospatial analyses (Chapter 15), including different strategies to describe biodiversity (Chapters 10–14).

This structure allows the book to be used either as a complete course or as a reference guide. For example, I often consult Chapter 5, especially when using the map function from the purrr package. Most chapters can be read independently, as each includes prerequisites or an introduction outlining its objectives.

However, if you are new to R, I recommend reading the chapters in order, especially Chapters 1 to 6. After that, you can move to the topics that interest you most. Chapter 1 also provides useful ideas for teaching and learning with the book, including suggestions for study groups. Chapter 2 introduces an interesting application of the hypothetical-deductive method using flowcharts to: (a) identify relevant variables, (b) improve sampling design, (c) facilitate statistical test selection, and (d) improve interpretation and communication of results. Chapters 4 and 5 are essential for beginners or those revisiting core R concepts. Chapter 5 introduces the tidyverse, a “design philosophy, grammar, and structure” of packages. Chapter 6 builds on this, introducing graphical grammar and preparing readers for more advanced analyses.

For those already familiar with R, Chapters 7 to 15 can be approached directly. Chapters 7 to 9 focus on statistical methods to explore relationships between variables, while Chapters 10 to 14 cover biodiversity metrics. Chapter 15 introduces geospatial data analysis in R, providing both practical tools and conceptual foundations.

Some argue that rapid technological change makes methodological books quickly obsolete. I do not believe this applies here. The methods presented are well established and widely used, while also including modern approaches. Having such a comprehensive and free resource in Portuguese is a privilege. I thank the authors for this contribution.

Tadeu Siqueira Institute of Biosciences São Paulo State University – UNESP
